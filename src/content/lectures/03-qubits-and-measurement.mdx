## From coin flips to qubits

Most algorithms are **deterministic**: same input, same output. **Probabilistic** (stochastic) algorithms can return different outputs, each with a probability, and the probabilities add to 1. Classical computers fake randomness with pseudo-random number generators. Quantum measurement is **genuinely** random.

A **probabilistic bit** is like a light switch in a dark room: it *is* on or off, we just don't know which. Say "0 with probability $P$, 1 with probability $1-P$". When we look, we update: seeing 0 sets $P$ to 100%. That probability only describes **our ignorance**.

## The qubit

Replace "0 or 1" with **two basis vectors**:

$$
\ket0 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \ket1 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

A qubit can be any unit-length combination of them:

$$
\ket\psi = \A{\alpha}\ket0 + \B{\beta}\ket1 = \begin{pmatrix}\A\alpha\\ \B\beta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\A\alpha|^2 + |\B\beta|^2 = 1.
$$

<Callout type="notation" title="Colours on this site">
$\A\alpha$ ("alpha", the amount of $\ket0$) is always **purple** and $\B\beta$ ("beta", the amount of $\ket1$) is always **teal**, in equations and in every picture. They are **amplitudes**: complex numbers, not probabilities.
</Callout>

**What α and β mean.** Measure "0 or 1" and you get 0 with probability $|\A\alpha|^2$ and 1 with probability $|\B\beta|^2$. Afterwards the qubit **is** whatever you saw: $\ket0$ or $\ket1$. That is why the condition $|\A\alpha|^2 + |\B\beta|^2 = 1$ is needed: probabilities must add to 1.

## Measuring in other ways: + or −

As well as "0 or 1", we can measure "**+ or −**", where

$$
\ket+ = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1), \qquad \ket- = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - \ket1).
$$

The $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ keeps them unit length: $(\tfrac1{\sqrt2})^2 + (\tfrac1{\sqrt2})^2 = 1$. Both $\ket+$ and $\ket-$ give 0 or 1 with 50% each. Yet, as we'll compute, measuring $\ket+$ in the +/− basis gives "+" **every time**. A classical bit can only be looked at one way; a qubit can be measured in infinitely many bases, which are superpositions of each other.

### The rule for any measurement

<Callout type="idea" title="The Born rule">
The probability that a system prepared in $\ket\psi$ is found in $\ket\phi$ is

$$
P(\phi|\psi) = |\braket{\phi}{\psi}|^2.
$$

Read it right to left: start in $\psi$, ask "is it $\phi$?". The inner product $\braket{\phi}{\psi}$ is the **amplitude**; square its size to get the **probability**.
</Callout>

Two sanity checks the lecture lists: asking whether a state is in **itself** gives $|\braket{\psi}{\psi}|^2 = 1$; asking whether it is in an **orthogonal** state (e.g. $\ket-$ when you prepared $\ket+$) gives 0.

### Deriving P(+|ψ)

<Callout type="notation">
Remember a bra is the conjugate transpose: $\bra+ = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\bra0 + \bra1)$, and the basis is **orthonormal**: $\braket00 = \braket11 = 1$, $\braket01 = \braket10 = 0$.
</Callout>

Step by step:

$$
\braket{+}{\psi} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\bra0 + \bra1)(\A\alpha\ket0 + \B\beta\ket1)
= \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\A\alpha\braket00 + \B\beta\braket01 + \A\alpha\braket10 + \B\beta\braket11\big)
= \tfrac{\A\alpha + \B\beta}{\sqrt2}.
$$

Square the size:

$$
P(+|\psi) = \frac{|\A\alpha + \B\beta|^2}{2}, \qquad P(-|\psi) = \frac{|\A\alpha - \B\beta|^2}{2}.
$$

<MeasureLab />

<Callout type="mistake">
Three slips account for most lost marks here. (1) Stopping at the amplitude $\tfrac{\alpha+\beta}{\sqrt2}$ and forgetting to **square**. (2) Squaring the parts separately: $|\alpha+\beta|^2 \ne |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2$ in general; that difference *is* interference. (3) Sign slips when β is negative: with $\beta = -0.8$, $\alpha - \beta = 0.6 - (-0.8) = 1.4$.
</Callout>

## Phase: what makes a qubit a qubit

Write each amplitude in polar form (Lecture 2): $\A\alpha = |\alpha|e^{i a}$, $\B\beta = |\beta|e^{i b}$. The angles are **phases**.

**Global phase does nothing.** Multiply the whole state by $e^{i\gamma}$: every probability gets multiplied by $|e^{i\gamma}|^2 = 1$. So $\ket\psi$ and $e^{i\gamma}\ket\psi$ are physically the **same state**. Example: $-\ket0$ is the same as $\ket0$.

**Relative phase matters.** $\ket+$ and $\ket-$ differ only by the sign (a phase of $\pi$) in front of $\ket1$, but $P(+|+) = 1$ while $P(+|-) = 0$. So we can always move all the phase onto $\ket1$ and write

$$
\ket\psi = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\ket0 + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\ket1.
$$

Two real numbers, $\theta$ and $\varphi$, describe any qubit (up to the irrelevant global phase).

## The Bloch sphere

Treat $\theta$ as latitude (measured down from the north pole) and $\varphi$ as longitude: every qubit is a point on a sphere.

- North pole: $\ket0$ ($\theta = 0$). South pole: $\ket1$ ($\theta = \pi$).
- Equator: equal superpositions. $\ket+$ at $\varphi = 0$, $\ket-$ at $\varphi = \pi$, $\ket{i} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + i\ket1)$ at $\varphi = \pi/2$, $\ket{-i}$ at $\varphi = 3\pi/2$.
- A measurement is an **axis** through the sphere. Orthogonal states sit at **opposite** points.

<Bloch />

Want to see it properly in 3D, rotate it and watch measurements as shadows? Open the [3D Bloch sphere](/visuals#bloch) and [interference as arrows](/visuals#clocks).

<Callout type="careful" title="Why θ/2, not θ?">
$\ket0$ and $\ket1$ are orthogonal but sit 180° apart on the sphere. Halving the angle makes 180° on the sphere into 90° between the vectors. So "opposite on the sphere" always means "orthogonal as vectors".
</Callout>

## Worked exercises

<Exercise title="3.1 · The in-class exercise: P(+|ψ) for four states" from="Lecture 3, in-class exercise">
Using $P(+|\psi) = \dfrac{|\alpha+\beta|^2}{2}$, find $P(+|\psi)$ when $\ket\psi$ is (a) $\ket+$, (b) $\ket-$, (c) $\ket1$, (d) $\ket0$.
<Steps>
1. Read off α and β for each state by matching with $\alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1$.
2. (a) $\ket+$: $\alpha = \beta = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, so $P = \dfrac{|\tfrac1{\sqrt2}+\tfrac1{\sqrt2}|^2}{2} = \dfrac{(\sqrt2)^2}{2} = 1$.
3. (b) $\ket-$: $\alpha = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, $\beta = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, so $P = \dfrac{|0|^2}{2} = 0$.
4. (c) $\ket1$: $\alpha = 0$, $\beta = 1$, so $P = \dfrac{|1|^2}{2} = \tfrac12$.
5. (d) $\ket0$: $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 0$, so $P = \tfrac12$.
</Steps>
<Answer>(a) 1, (b) 0, (c) ½, (d) ½. The lesson: $\ket0$ and $\ket1$ both give 50% for +/−, just as $\ket\pm$ both give 50% for 0/1. Different states, same 50% in one basis; phase tells them apart in another.</Answer>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="3.2 · A quiz-style question" from="Problem set style">
$\ket\psi = 0.6\ket0 - 0.8\ket1$. Check it is normalised and find $P(0)$, $P(1)$, $P(+)$ and $P(-)$.
<Steps>
1. Normalised? $|0.6|^2 + |-0.8|^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1$ ✓.
2. $P(0) = |\alpha|^2 = 0.36$, $P(1) = |\beta|^2 = 0.64$.
3. $P(+) = \dfrac{|0.6 + (-0.8)|^2}{2} = \dfrac{(-0.2)^2}{2} = \dfrac{0.04}{2} = 0.02$.
4. $P(-) = \dfrac{|0.6 - (-0.8)|^2}{2} = \dfrac{1.4^2}{2} = \dfrac{1.96}{2} = 0.98$.
5. Check: $0.02 + 0.98 = 1$ ✓.
</Steps>
<Answer>$P(0) = 0.36$, $P(1) = 0.64$, $P(+) = 0.02$, $P(-) = 0.98$. The state is almost $\ket-$.</Answer>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="3.3 · Complex amplitudes" from="Extension">
$\ket\psi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket0 + \tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\ket1$. Find $P(+)$ and $P(i)$ where $\ket{i} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + i\ket1)$.
<Steps>
1. $P(+) = \dfrac{|\tfrac1{\sqrt2} + \tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}|^2}{2} = \dfrac{\tfrac12|1+i|^2}{2} = \dfrac{\tfrac12\cdot 2}{2} = \tfrac12$.
2. For $P(i)$ build the bra, **conjugating**: $\bra{i} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\bra0 - i\bra1)$.
3. $\braket{i}{\psi} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\tfrac1{\sqrt2} - i\cdot\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\big) = \tfrac12(1 - i^2) = \tfrac12\cdot 2 = 1$.
4. $P(i) = |1|^2 = 1$: of course, $\ket\psi$ *is* $\ket i$.
</Steps>
<Answer>$P(+) = \tfrac12$, $P(i) = 1$. Forgetting the conjugate in step 2 would give $\tfrac12(1 + i^2) = 0$, the opposite (wrong) answer.</Answer>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="3.4 · Is this a valid state? Fix it" from="Problem set style">
Is $\ket\phi = \ket0 + 2i\ket1$ a valid qubit state? If not, normalise it.
<Steps>
1. $|1|^2 + |2i|^2 = 1 + 4 = 5 \ne 1$, so not valid.
2. Divide by the norm $\sqrt5$: $\ket\phi = \tfrac1{\sqrt5}\ket0 + \tfrac{2i}{\sqrt5}\ket1$.
3. Check: $\tfrac15 + \tfrac45 = 1$ ✓.
</Steps>
<Answer>Not valid as written; normalised it is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(\ket0 + 2i\ket1)$, with $P(0) = 0.2$, $P(1) = 0.8$.</Answer>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="3.5 · Global vs relative phase" from="Lecture 3: Bloch sphere">
Are $\ket a = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - \ket1)$ and $\ket b = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-\ket0 + \ket1)$ the same state? What about $\ket a$ and $\ket+$?
<Steps>
1. $\ket b = -1\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - \ket1) = -\ket a = e^{i\pi}\ket a$: a **global** phase.
2. So $\ket a$ and $\ket b$ give identical probabilities for every measurement: same physical state.
3. $\ket a = \ket-$ and $\ket+$ differ by a **relative** phase (sign on $\ket1$ only), and $P(+|-) = 0$.
</Steps>
<Answer>$\ket a$ and $\ket b$: same state. $\ket a$ and $\ket +$: different, in fact perfectly distinguishable.</Answer>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="3.6 · Find θ and φ" from="Lecture 3: Bloch sphere">
Find the Bloch angles of $\ket\psi = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\ket0 + \tfrac{1}{2}e^{i\pi/4}\ket1$.
<Steps>
1. Match with $\cos\tfrac\theta2\ket0 + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\ket1$.
2. $\cos\tfrac\theta2 = \tfrac{\sqrt3}2 \Rightarrow \tfrac\theta2 = \tfrac\pi6 \Rightarrow \theta = \tfrac\pi3$ (60°).
3. Check $\sin\tfrac\pi6 = \tfrac12$ ✓, and $\varphi = \tfrac\pi4$ (45°).
</Steps>
<Answer>$\theta = 60°$, $\varphi = 45°$: in the northern hemisphere (more likely 0 than 1), turned 45° round from $\ket+$ towards $\ket i$.</Answer>
</Exercise>

<Deeper title="Density matrices and mixed states" level="MSc">
A probabilistic mixture "$\ket0$ with probability ½, $\ket1$ with ½" is **not** the same as $\ket+$, even though both give 50/50 for 0/1. To describe mixtures, use the **density matrix** $\rho = \sum_k p_k \ket{\psi_k}\bra{\psi_k}$. The mixture gives $\rho = \tfrac12 I$, while $\ket+$ gives $\ket+\bra+ = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$: the off-diagonal **coherences** are the relative phase. Probabilities become $P(\phi) = \bra\phi\rho\ket\phi = \mathrm{Tr}(\rho\ket\phi\bra\phi)$. On the Bloch sphere, mixed states fill the **inside** of the ball: $\rho = \tfrac12(I + x X + y Y + z Z)$ with $x^2+y^2+z^2\le1$. You will meet $I/2$ again in Lecture 9 as "what Bob holds before Alice's message". Nielsen & Chuang §2.4.
</Deeper>

<Deeper title="Measurement as projectors, and POVMs" level="MSc">
A measurement in an orthonormal basis $\{\ket{k}\}$ is described by projectors $P_k = \ket k\bra k$ with $\sum_k P_k = I$. Outcome $k$ has probability $\bra\psi P_k\ket\psi$ and leaves the state $P_k\ket\psi/\sqrt{\bra\psi P_k\ket\psi}$. The most general measurement (a **POVM**) uses positive operators $E_k$ with $\sum E_k = I$, which need not be projectors; it can have more outcomes than the dimension. Example: the three-outcome "trine" measurement on a qubit. Nielsen & Chuang §2.2.3–2.2.6.
</Deeper>

<Deeper title="Is the randomness really fundamental?" level="PhD">
Bell's theorem (Lecture 8) shows no *local* hidden-variable model reproduces quantum statistics, which is what makes "certified randomness" possible: a Bell violation proves the outputs could not have been predetermined (Pironio et al., Nature 464, 1021, 2010). Bohmian mechanics is deterministic but non-local, so the randomness question becomes "does the theory need non-locality?".
</Deeper>
