## Recap: the question Bell answered

Entangled states can't be written as a product, and measuring one half instantly changes the other's state (Lecture 5). Einstein concluded quantum mechanics was incomplete: there must be a **local hidden-variable (LHV) theory** underneath in which particles "know" their results in advance. For 30 years nobody could see any experimental difference between the two pictures. Bell (1964) found one; Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt (CHSH, 1969) turned it into the form we use.

<Callout type="idea" title="The plan for this lecture">
1. Assume an LHV theory and derive a limit: $|S| \le 2$.
2. Compute what quantum mechanics predicts: $|S|$ up to $2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83$.
3. Do the experiment. Nature gives about 2.8. So at least one LHV assumption is false.
</Callout>

## The Bohm–EPR experiment

A source emits pairs of entangled spin-½ particles (or photons). One goes to Alice's spin meter, one to Bob's.

- Alice measures her particle along direction $a_0$ **or** $a_1$ (her choice, each run).
- Bob measures along $b_0$ **or** $b_1$.
- Every measurement gives $+1$ or $-1$ (in units of $\hbar/2$).

They repeat many times and then compare results to look for correlations.

<Callout type="notation" title="Correlation E(a, b)">
For a run $i$ with settings $a, b$, write the results as $A_i, B_i\in\{+1,-1\}$. Their product $A_iB_i$ is $+1$ if they agree and $-1$ if they differ. The **correlation** is its average:

$$
E(a, b) = \frac1N\sum_i A_iB_i = P(\text{same}) - P(\text{different}).
$$

$E = +1$: always agree. $E = -1$: always disagree. $E = 0$: no correlation.
</Callout>

## Making a Bell inequality

**Assume an LHV theory.** Then every pair leaves the source carrying definite answers for **all four** settings, whether or not they are measured: $A_0$ and $A_1$ for Alice's two settings, $B_0$ and $B_1$ for Bob's, each $\pm1$. Locality means Alice's answer doesn't depend on Bob's setting, and vice versa.

For one pair, define

$$
\gamma = A_0B_0 + A_1B_0 + A_0B_1 - A_1B_1 = A_0(B_0 + B_1) + A_1(B_0 - B_1).
$$

Since $B_0, B_1 = \pm1$, either they are equal (then $B_0+B_1 = \pm2$ and $B_0-B_1 = 0$) or opposite (then $B_0+B_1 = 0$ and $B_0 - B_1 = \pm2$). Either way exactly one bracket survives, times $A_0$ or $A_1 = \pm1$. So

$$
\gamma = \pm 2 \quad\text{for every pair.}
$$

Averaging over $N$ pairs, the average of numbers that are each $\pm2$ lies between $-2$ and $2$:

$$
|S| = \big|E(a_0,b_0) + E(a_1,b_0) + E(a_0,b_1) - E(a_1,b_1)\big| \le 2.
$$

This is the **CHSH inequality**.

## Violating it with quantum mechanics

### Quantum correlations

In quantum mechanics, a spin measurement along direction $\hat a$ is the observable $\sigma_a = \hat a\cdot\vec\sigma = a_xX + a_yY + a_zZ$ (eigenvalues $\pm1$). The correlation is an **expectation value**:

$$
E(a, b) = \bra\Psi\sigma_a\otimes\sigma_b\ket\Psi.
$$

Use the singlet $\ket{\Psi^-} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} - \ket{10})$. It looks the same in every basis (it's anti-correlated along every axis; Lecture 5, exercise 5.4). So we can choose Alice's axis to be $z$ without loss of generality, and put Bob's at angle $\theta$ to it in the x–z plane:

$$
\sigma_a = Z, \qquad \sigma_b = \cos\theta\, Z + \sin\theta\, X.
$$

Then $E = \cos\theta\bra{\Psi^-}Z\otimes Z\ket{\Psi^-} + \sin\theta\bra{\Psi^-}Z\otimes X\ket{\Psi^-}$, and the exercise below shows the first is $-1$ and the second $0$:

$$
E(a,b) = -\cos\theta.
$$

Same direction ($\theta = 0$): always opposite results. Perpendicular ($90°$): no correlation.

### Choosing the angles

Take $a_0 = 0°$, $a_1 = 90°$, $b_0 = 45°$, $b_1 = -45°$:

| Pair | Angle | $E = -\cos\theta$ |
|---|---|---|
| $a_0, b_0$ | 45° | $-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ |
| $a_1, b_0$ | 45° | $-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ |
| $a_0, b_1$ | 45° | $-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ |
| $a_1, b_1$ | 135° | $+\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ |

$$
S = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2} - \tfrac1{\sqrt2} - \tfrac1{\sqrt2} - \tfrac1{\sqrt2} = -\tfrac{4}{\sqrt2} = -2\sqrt2 \approx -2.83.
$$

$|S| = 2\sqrt2 > 2$: **the Bell inequality is violated.** Cirel'son (Tsirelson, 1980) proved no quantum state or measurement can do better.

<Callout type="mistake">
Two easy slips. (1) The minus sign belongs to $E(a_1, b_1)$, the **fourth** term only; check which term your source puts it on (some books order the terms differently, but the minus is always on exactly one). (2) $E = -\cos\theta$ is for the **singlet** $\ket{\Psi^-}$. For $\ket{\Phi^+}$ with settings in the x–z plane, $E = +\cos(\theta_a - \theta_b)$, so the signs (and the best angles) change, though the maximum $|S|$ is still $2\sqrt2$.
</Callout>

## Experimental tests

- **1972, Clauser & Freedman**: first violation, with photons. But the settings were fixed before the pairs were made, so in principle the source could "know" them.
- **1980–82, Aspect et al.**: settings switched rapidly *while the photons were in flight*, closing much of the locality gap.
- **Loopholes** still left open, each a way an LHV model could fake a violation:
  - **locality**: one side's setting or result could reach the other in time;
  - **memory**: hidden variables could depend on earlier runs;
  - **detection**: if many particles go undetected, the detected ones may be a biased sample;
  - **coincidence**: deciding which detections count as "pairs" can bias the result.
- **2015, loophole-free tests**: Delft (Hensen et al., electron spins 1.3 km apart), Vienna (Giustina et al., Zeilinger's group) and NIST (Shalm et al.) closed locality and detection loopholes together.
- **2022 Nobel Prize in Physics**: Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger.

## What exactly does Bell rule out? The assumptions

Deriving $|S|\le2$ quietly used several assumptions. A violation means **at least one** fails, and which one is a matter of interpretation.

1. **Hidden variables**: results are fixed by some (possibly unknown) variable $\lambda$ the pair carries.
2. **Factorisability**, made of two parts:
   - **Locality** (no superluminal influence): Alice's result depends only on $\lambda$ and her own setting, not Bob's. Special relativity forbids signalling, though some (Shimony, Redhead) consider a weaker non-local "passion at a distance" that can't be used to signal.
   - **Statistical independence**: the distribution of $\lambda$ doesn't depend on which settings are chosen. Dropping this is **superdeterminism** (Bell 1977; Hossenfelder & Palmer 2020).
3. **Comparing different settings**: the inequality combines results for four setting pairs, but each pair is only measured with one. Either:
   - **statistically**: assume runs with different settings sample the same distribution of $\lambda$. This is what experiments do, but it needs everything except the setting held fixed; Bell's "moons of Jupiter" remark is that you can't control every external factor, so $\lambda$ might vary with the run $i$;
   - or **counterfactually**: assume unmeasured results still *have* values (**counterfactual definiteness**). They need not be measurable, just exist.

<Callout type="careful" title="A word about the word non-locality">
Quantum-information people often call any Bell violation "non-locality", even when they don't mean the locality assumption specifically failed. Dr Hance's slides flag this; keep the assumptions separate in exam answers.
</Callout>

## Worked exercises

<Exercise title="8.1 · Prove E = −cos θ for the singlet" from="Lecture 8 exercise: prove this">
Show $\bra{\Psi^-}Z\otimes Z\ket{\Psi^-} = -1$ and $\bra{\Psi^-}Z\otimes X\ket{\Psi^-} = 0$, hence $E = -\cos\theta$.
<Steps>
1. $Z\otimes Z$ multiplies $\ket{ab}$ by $(-1)^a(-1)^b$: $\ket{01}\to-\ket{01}$, $\ket{10}\to-\ket{10}$.
2. So $(Z\otimes Z)\ket{\Psi^-} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-\ket{01}+\ket{10}) = -\ket{\Psi^-}$, giving $\bra{\Psi^-}Z\otimes Z\ket{\Psi^-} = -1$.
3. $Z\otimes X$: X flips the second bit, Z gives $(-1)^{a}$ for the first: $\ket{01}\to\ket{00}$, $\ket{10}\to-\ket{11}$.
4. So $(Z\otimes X)\ket{\Psi^-} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})$, which is orthogonal to $\ket{\Psi^-}$ (no $\ket{01}$ or $\ket{10}$ terms): expectation 0.
5. $E = \cos\theta\cdot(-1) + \sin\theta\cdot 0 = -\cos\theta$.
</Steps>
<Answer>$E(a,b) = -\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between Alice's and Bob's directions.</Answer>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="8.2 · Compute S for other angles" from="Problem set style">
For the singlet, compute S with all four settings along the same axis ($a_0=a_1=b_0=b_1 = 0°$), and with $a_0 = 0°, a_1 = 90°, b_0 = 0°, b_1 = 90°$.
<Steps>
1. All equal: every $E = -\cos0 = -1$, so $S = -1 - 1 - 1 - (-1) = -2$. $|S| = 2$: right at the limit, no violation.
2. Second set: $E(a_0,b_0) = -\cos0 = -1$; $E(a_1,b_0) = -\cos90° = 0$; $E(a_0,b_1) = -\cos(-90°) = 0$; $E(a_1,b_1) = -\cos0 = -1$.
3. $S = -1 + 0 + 0 - (-1) = 0$.
</Steps>
<Answer>$S = -2$ and $S = 0$. Entanglement alone isn't enough; you need well-chosen settings, which is what the 45° offsets achieve.</Answer>
</Exercise>

<Exercise title="8.3 · Why S can't beat 2 classically, by brute force" from="Lecture 8: results table">
How many deterministic strategies $(A_0,A_1,B_0,B_1)$ are there, and what values can $\gamma$ take?
<Steps>
1. Each of the four is $\pm1$: $2^4 = 16$ strategies.
2. For each, $\gamma = A_0(B_0+B_1) + A_1(B_0-B_1)$; one bracket is 0 and the other $\pm2$, so $\gamma\in\{-2, +2\}$.
3. A randomised LHV model just mixes these 16 strategies, and any average of numbers in $[-2, 2]$ stays in $[-2, 2]$.
</Steps>
<Answer>16 strategies, $\gamma = \pm2$ for all of them, so $|S|\le2$ for any LHV model.</Answer>
</Exercise>

<Deeper title="Tsirelson's bound in three lines" level="MSc">
Let $\hat A_0, \hat A_1, \hat B_0, \hat B_1$ be observables with eigenvalues $\pm1$ (so each squares to $I$), Alice's commuting with Bob's. Define $\hat C = \hat A_0(\hat B_0 + \hat B_1) + \hat A_1(\hat B_0 - \hat B_1)$. Then $\hat C^2 = 4I - [\hat A_0, \hat A_1][\hat B_0, \hat B_1]$. Each commutator has norm at most 2, so $\|\hat C^2\|\le 8$ and $|\langle\hat C\rangle|\le 2\sqrt2$. Classically everything commutes, the commutator term vanishes, and you get 2 back. (Landau 1987; Cirel'son 1980.)
</Deeper>

<Deeper title="Beyond CHSH: GHZ, Hardy, and contextuality" level="MSc">
Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (1989): with three qubits in $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{000}+\ket{111})$, quantum mechanics predicts outcomes that LHVs can't match **even once**: no statistics needed. Hardy's paradox does something similar with two qubits. The Kochen–Specker theorem shows a related no-go for *non-contextual* hidden variables, even without spatial separation. Mermin, "Hidden variables and the two theorems of John Bell", Rev. Mod. Phys. 65, 803 (1993) is a superb read.
</Deeper>

<Deeper title="Device independence and self-testing" level="PhD">
A maximal violation $|S| = 2\sqrt2$ certifies, up to local changes of basis, that the devices share a singlet and measure along the optimal axes: **self-testing** (Mayers–Yao; Šupić & Bowles, Quantum 4, 337, 2020). This underlies device-independent QKD (demonstrated 2022 by Nadlinger et al. and Zhang et al.) and certified randomness. The 2020 MIP* = RE result (Ji, Natarajan, Vidick, Wright, Yuen) showed that the complexity of non-local games is astonishingly rich, resolving Connes' embedding problem.
</Deeper>
