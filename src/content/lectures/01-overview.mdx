## Five myths

The lecture starts with five things people tend to believe about quantum computers. Most of them are wrong.

**Myth 1: they will solve NP-complete problems efficiently.** *Wrong.* NP-complete problems (like the travelling salesman problem) have no known efficient algorithm, classical or quantum, and most researchers expect none exists. Quantum computers speed up *specific* structured problems.

**Myth 2: they are too complicated to ever be built.** *Wrong.* Working (noisy) machines are on the cloud right now: IBM's and others. We are in the **NISQ** era (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum): around a thousand physical qubits, but too noisy for long computations. Fault tolerance needs error correction, which costs many physical qubits per reliable "logical" qubit.

**Myth 3: it is just another fast architecture, like a GPU.** *Wrong.* A GPU uses quantum physics in its transistors but still processes ordinary bits. A quantum computer processes **qubits**, a fundamentally different kind of information with different allowed operations.

**Myth 4: it is hard and you need quantum physics.** *Kind of true.* But the maths is linear algebra with complex numbers: no calculus. Circuit diagrams make it even simpler to read.

**Myth 5: quantum and classical computers solve the same problems.** *True.* Each can simulate the other, so the set of *solvable* problems is identical. The difference is **efficiency**: for some problems (factoring, simulating molecules) the best known quantum algorithm is exponentially faster than the best known classical one.

## Why physics needed quantum mechanics

### Waves: the double slit

Thomas Young shone light through two narrow slits onto a screen. One slit gives a single bright blob (a bell curve). Two slits do **not** give two blobs; they give alternating bright and dark **bands**. That pattern, **interference**, is what waves do: where two crests meet they add up, where a crest meets a trough they cancel. So light is a wave.

### Particles: the photoelectric effect

Shine light on a metal and electrons can be knocked out. Surprise: below a certain **frequency** (colour), no electrons come out *however bright* the light is. Einstein explained it: light arrives in indivisible lumps, **photons**, each with energy set by its frequency, and an electron can only absorb one lump. So light is also a particle. (This, not relativity, is what Einstein's Nobel Prize was for.)

<Callout type="idea" title="The resolution">
Treat things as particles (single detection events), but let the **probability** of where they turn up be governed by something that behaves like a wave and can interfere. That something is the **wavefunction**.
</Callout>

### The wavefunction

<Callout type="notation">
$\psi$ (Greek "psi") is the wavefunction. For a quantum system, $\psi(a)$ is a complex number for each possible outcome $a$, and the probability of finding outcome $a$ is

$$
P(a) = |\psi(a)|^2.
$$

The bars $|\cdot|$ mean "size of" a complex number (Lecture 2). In this module the wavefunction becomes a short list of numbers: the **state vector** of a qubit.
</Callout>

In 1926 Schrödinger wrote down how an isolated system's wavefunction changes in time:

$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = \hat H \psi.
$$

You do not need to use this equation. The point: components with different energies (given by the **Hamiltonian** $\hat H$) evolve at different rates and so **interfere**, like waves. In quantum computing we never solve this equation directly; we describe its effect as **gates** (matrices).

### Operators, commutators and uncertainty

Measurable properties (position, momentum, spin…) are represented by **operators**, things that act on the wavefunction. Order matters: measuring position then momentum is not the same as momentum then position. The **commutator** measures how much:

$$
[\hat A, \hat B] = \hat A\hat B - \hat B\hat A.
$$

For position and momentum $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$, which leads to **Heisenberg's uncertainty relation** (1927):

$$
\Delta x\,\Delta p \ \ge\ \frac{\hbar}{2}.
$$

You cannot know both to arbitrary precision for the same system.

### Measurement changes things

Von Neumann (1932) formalised measurement: after you measure, you update the wavefunction **by hand** to the state matching the result you saw. So quantum systems evolve in two different ways: smoothly (Schrödinger) when not observed, and by a sudden jump when measured. Lecture 3 turns this into precise rules for qubits.

## From physics to computing

- **1980s.** Benioff, Feynman and Deutsch describe quantum computers. Feynman's point: simulating nature on a classical computer seems exponentially hard, so build the simulator out of quantum parts.
- **1994.** Peter Shor's factoring algorithm: an exponential speedup over the best known classical algorithm, and a threat to RSA encryption.
- **1996.** Lov Grover's search algorithm: a quadratic speedup for unstructured search.
- **1995–96.** Shor and Steane invent quantum error correction, showing noisy qubits can in principle be protected.
- **Today.** IBM, Google and others run cloud quantum computers; governments invest billions; applications explored include chemistry, materials, drug design, optimisation and machine learning.

<Deeper title="What 'exponentially faster' really means" level="Module+">
Complexity theorists name the problems a quantum computer solves efficiently (with bounded error) **BQP**. Known facts: $\mathrm{P} \subseteq \mathrm{BPP} \subseteq \mathrm{BQP} \subseteq \mathrm{PSPACE}$. Factoring is in BQP (Shor) but is not known to be NP-complete, and it is not proven to be outside P either. So "exponential speedup" always means *over the best known classical algorithm*. Proven separations exist only in restricted models (oracle or query settings, and shallow circuits, e.g. Bravyi, Gosset and König 2018). Good next read: Scott Aaronson, *Quantum Computing Since Democritus* (2013), chapters 10–15.
</Deeper>

<Deeper title="Interpretations: what is the wavefunction?" level="MSc">
The lecture defines the wavefunction pragmatically, as "the thing we get probabilities from". Whether it is *real* (ontic) or *knowledge* (epistemic) is an open question: Copenhagen, many-worlds (Everett, which Deutsch championed), Bohmian mechanics, QBism and collapse models all agree on predictions. The 2012 Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem shows that, under reasonable assumptions, the quantum state cannot be purely epistemic. Your lecturer Dr Hance works on quantum foundations, so this is a good office-hours topic.
</Deeper>
